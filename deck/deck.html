02 — The problemvexen

A prompt is not a permission.

The typical production agent holds a long-lived credential with roughly an employee’s scope, because narrowing it was fiddly and nobody had time. Its limits live in the prompt, where they are advisory.

SYSTEM_PROMPT = """ You are a purchasing agent. Never spend more than $100. Always ask before refunding. """ # ↑ these are suggestions. # nothing enforces them. # nothing records the attempt.
Failure 1 — most common

The confident mistake

Not attacked, not jailbroken — just wrong. Buys 500 units instead of 5 because a price was per-case. Refunds twice because the first had not settled. Almost entirely preventable with limits.

Failure 2 — adversarial

The manipulated agent

Indirect prompt injection hides in a page, a PDF, a support ticket. Every published model-layer defence has been bypassed. You cannot fix this inside the thing being attacked.

Failure 3 — why pilots stall

The accountability vacuum

What may this agent do, right now? What was refused? Who approved the exception, and can you prove the record is unedited? No answers means no production deployment.

05 / 18
05 — The insightvexen

The actor does not get to decide the limits of its own authority.

Banks, hospitals, aviation and payment processors all separate three things that agent deployments currently fuse into a single model call.

1 — Identity

Who is asking

The agent, with its own key. Never shared, never ambiguous in the record.

2 — Policy

What they may do

Decided elsewhere, by someone else, in a workspace the agent cannot read or write. This is the piece that is missing today.

3 — Execution

What happens

The side effect — but only after policy has answered.

A bank teller can be completely honest and still not be permitted to move ten million dollars alone. At scale you cannot verify intent. You can only constrain consequence.

The teller does not set their own withdrawal limit. The pilot does not grant their own clearance.

08 — The hard partvexen

Approvals that actually hold.

Most human-in-the-loop implementations get one or two of these five. Missing any single one gives you an approval that can be replayed, escalated or forged — worse than no approval at all, because now a signed-looking record says a human agreed.

01

Single-use

Consumed the moment it is spent. No replay, ever.

02

Short-lived

30 minutes by default. Yesterday’s yes authorises nothing today.

03

Atomically claimed

Two concurrent requests can never both spend it.

04

Scope-bound

Same permission, same order, same amount to the cent.

05

Zero client trust

State loads server-side. Caller claims are ignored.

verified: replay refused verified: amount escalation refused verified: cross-order reuse refused verified: forged token refused

09 — For the person accountablevexen

Two taps. No login. Fifteen seconds.

A control humans find annoying gets switched off, and a switched-off control protects nobody. So convenience is treated as a security requirement, not a nicety.

The subtlety that shaped the design

A link that acts on GET is a vulnerability.

Mail providers and corporate link scanners fetch every URL in a message to check it. If Approve happened on GET, your security scanner would approve the $40,000 purchase before you opened the email. So the GET is strictly read-only and the decision requires a deliberate POST.

HMAC-SHA256, domain-separated ids only — never a session 24-hour expiry minted per recipient

SHA-256 over every event’s identifying fields. A verification pass recomputes each hash and names the rows that changed, if any.

So “our agent did not do that” and “a human approved this on the 14th” become checkable claims rather than assertions. This is the feature that comes up first in every serious conversation.

12 — In practicevexen

$740 of packaging, at three in the morning.

A three-person e-commerce company. The purchasing agent holds a payment key and a supplier login.

Before

Nobody is checking

The agent does whatever the model decides overnight. The founder finds out from her bank.

Setup — 60 seconds

Deploy & one rule

One click from the library: up to $200, three approved suppliers, $2,000 daily cap. She types “never let this agent touch payment credentials”.

Integration — one line

Guard the function

Her engineer adds a decorator above the function that actually places the order. If Vexen says no, it never runs.

3:04am

Halted, then allowed once

$740 attempt stops. Her phone asks. She taps approve. It goes through — once, that amount, that order. Replay tomorrow is refused.

Nothing in that story requires her to understand an API key, and nothing in it requires her to trust the agent. Two weeks later her accountant asks about the $740, and she hands over a record with a hash proving nobody edited it.
