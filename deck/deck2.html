02
The problem
Vexen / 03

A prompt is not a permission.

Today an agent's limits are a sentence someone typed into a system prompt. Nothing outside the agent verifies it. Nothing halts the action. Nothing proves what happened.

# the entire control system, today SYSTEM_PROMPT = """ You are a purchasing agent. Never spend more than $100. Always ask before refunding. """
Failure 1

No external check

The actor enforces its own limit. One prompt injection, one bad tool response, one hallucinated total — and the constraint evaporates silently.

Failure 2

No approval that holds

Hand-rolled approvals are replayable and amount-escalatable. A $10 approval gets reused for $10,000 — worse than no approval, because a signed-looking record now claims a human agreed.

Failure 3

No answer to “who allowed this?”

Evidence is scattered across model traces, vendor dashboards and application logs. Reconstruction takes hours, and nothing is tamper-evident.

03
What it costs today
Vexen / 04

Every team picks one of two bad options.

Option A

Keep the agent in read-only demo mode

Human confirms everything, or the agent never touches a real system. Safe, and it forfeits the entire reason the agent was built. The automation ROI never arrives — expensive, quietly, forever.

Option B

Hand it a real key and hope

Full credentials, prompt-level limits, no external stop. A risk that cannot be capped, priced, or explained to an auditor. Expensive once — and unexplainable afterwards.

The blocker on agents in production is no longer capability. It is authority — and authority is an infrastructure problem, not a model problem.

05
The insight
Vexen / 06

You cannot verify intent. You can only constrain consequence.

We never solved this for people by making people more trustworthy. We solved it with external limits: spending authority, dual control, segregation of duties, audit trails. A completely honest bank teller still cannot move ten million dollars alone.

Where the industry is looking

Make the model more trustworthy

Alignment, better prompts, evals, guardrails, content filters. All useful. None of it stops an action, and all of it puts a probabilistic system in charge of its own limits.

Where the answer is

Make the authority external and provable

Deterministic permissions the agent does not control, human approval on consequential actions, and a tamper-evident record. Boring infrastructure, which is exactly why it works.

The actor does not get to decide the limits of its own authority.

One decorator above the function that performs the side effect. Vexen decides before anything happens — and if Vexen is unreachable, the function does not run.

07
How the decision works
Vexen / 08

Deterministic by design. No model in the enforcement path.

A 13-step, default-deny evaluation with no I/O and no inference. The same request always returns the same answer, and the trace is the explanation.

01 — 03
Identify agent, workspace, action
04 — 06
Status, expiry, permission grant
07 — 09
Limits, resource and action scope
10 — 12
Org rules, approval state, claim
13
Emit decision, reason code, trace
Default deny

Anything not explicitly granted is refused. New capability is never silently permitted.

AI writes rules, never enforces

Plain English becomes a schema-validated rule, shown back before it takes effect. The model never touches the decision.

Explainable by construction

Every answer carries a machine-readable reason code and the full evaluation path.

The hard part
Vexen / 09

Approvals that actually hold.

This is where most implementations quietly fail. A half-correct approval is worse than none, because a signed-looking record then claims a human agreed.

Permission layers are the stickiest line in the stack, because removing one means choosing to run an ungoverned system. Nobody does that to save money.

Why we win
Vexen / 20

Four defences, in increasing order of durability.

1 — Position

On the write path, not the read path

Observability sits beside the action; Vexen sits in front of it. Once a guarded call is in production code, removing it is a decision to run ungoverned.

2 — Correctness

Invariants that take real adversarial work

Atomic single-use claims, cent-level scope binding, scan-safe POST-only actions, hash-verified audit, fail-closed defaults. Easy to demo, hard to actually get right.

3 — Both personas

The engineer integrates, the accountable human signs

Policy engines serve only developers, so nobody with a budget can use them. Plain-English rules and two-tap approvals put the buyer inside the product.

4 — Standard

The credential broker, then the artefact

Short-lived scoped credentials make bypass impossible. Then a portable, verifiable statement of bounded authority that travels between companies — the primitive agent commerce and agent insurance both need, and neither has.

Risks, stated first
Vexen / 23

The four things most likely to kill this.

Risk — no forcing function

No job title owns “agent authority”. Engineers feel a permission layer as friction; the exposed executive does not know the exposure exists.

Mitigation

Sell the incident, not the feature: show a buyer what their agents are currently permitted to do. The audit artefact is what an auditor, insurer or regulator asks for — and those three are arriving.

Risk — bypassable today

The credential broker is designed, not built, so a rigorous buyer says this is a convention rather than a control and waits.

Mitigation

It is the first thing this round funds, with a founding engineer out of IAM or payments hired to own it. Fair objection, dated answer.

Risk — incumbent bundling

Okta, Stripe or an agent framework ships a “good enough” governance feature.

Mitigation

Bundled features stop at logging and pausing. Correct approval invariants, scan-safe delivery and tamper-evident audit are the hard 20% nobody bundles — and an open spec makes us the reference, not the competitor.

Risk — solo founder, pre-revenue

Concentration risk, no enterprise sales experience, and a business that has never charged anyone.

Mitigation

55% of this round is two hires that close exactly those gaps. Runway is not the constraint: $50K in the bank against ~$200/month burn.

Every gap here is one I would rather name than have found in diligence.
